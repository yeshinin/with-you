The care gap at home

Families are doing the work of nurses, mostly without training.

63M Americans, about 1 in 4 adults, are now family caregivers.1
+45% growth in the number of family caregivers from 2015 to 2025.1
55% of caregivers supporting adults perform medical or nursing tasks: medicines, wound care, monitoring, equipment.1
22% of those caregivers say they received any training to do it.1
1 in 5 patients experience an adverse event after leaving the hospital.3
62% of adults 65+ live with two or more chronic conditions (26.4% of all U.S. adults).4
1 in 5 Americans will be 65 or older by 2030.5

Half of family caregivers already perform complicated tasks like injections, tube feedings and managing medical equipment, often with little or no preparation.2 The need is growing every year.

The same discharge, two very different weeks

Recovery happens at home, in the days nobody plans for.

One patient. One caregiver. Discharged on a Friday with a folder of instructions.

Without support

  1. FridayA thick folder of paperwork, explained once in a hurry, in a language that isn’t quite theirs.
  2. Day 2Five new medicines. Which ones with food? The caregiver guesses.
  3. Day 4The wound dressing looks different. Is that normal? No one to ask at 10pm.
  4. Day 6No ride to the follow-up appointment. It quietly gets missed.
  5. Day 12A 15-minute visit. The question that mattered most is forgotten.

With You

  1. Before leavingThe plan is explained in their language. The caregiver practises the dressing change before going home.
  2. Day 2A gentle voice walks through the morning medicines, one at a time, and waits for “done”.
  3. Day 4A picture guide shows what healing should look like, and when to call, and whom.
  4. Day 5“Do you have a ride to tomorrow’s appointment?” No, so a transport resource is lined up in time.
  5. Day 12A one-page summary of concerns and questions, ready before the visit.

How it works

Six ways With You stays with you.

Read itSee itHear itIn your language

It meets you where and how you want. If you prefer to read, we read. If pictures help, we show. If talking is easier, in your own language, we talk.

01

Understand what happened and what comes next

  • Starting during admission, your care is explained in words you understand, so you can take part in decisions.
  • Discharge instructions become daily guidance: what to do, when, and whom to contact.
  • Information in manageable amounts, with more detail whenever you want it.
02

Get help carrying out care at home

  • Voice prompts talk you through medicines, measurements and care tasks, giving you time to finish each step and confirm it.
  • Visual instructions for unfamiliar clinical tasks, based on your care team’s guidance.
  • Patients and caregivers learn tasks before leaving, with community health workers to help afterwards where available.
03

Prepare for everyday life throughout recovery

  • A personalised timeline of what to expect each week, with clinical guidance validated by your care team.
  • Learn what recovery looked like for people like you: your age, culture, conditions and living situation.
  • Practical details: bathing, sleeping, eating, movement, equipment, relationships and returning to work.
04

Spot practical barriers before they disrupt care

  • Asks whether you can actually carry out the plan: get medicines, arrange transport, attend appointments, find help at home.
  • Connects you with people and resources early enough to make arrangements.
  • Follows up on what’s unresolved, so asking for help leads to getting help.
05

Make the most of every care conversation

  • Capture experiences, concerns and questions through simple voice conversations, with no manual diary to keep.
  • Before each appointment, get a clear summary, your priority concerns and suggested questions.
  • Find the words to explain your needs, so fewer important concerns are lost to memory or time.
06

Find connection as your needs change

  • Connect with peers who understand similar experiences.
  • Optional milestone stories to read, watch or hear, clearly separated from clinical advice.
  • Support that continues after treatment ends, for however long your journey takes.

Clinical guidance comes from your care team and is reviewed by qualified people. Peer stories are always labelled as experience, not medical advice.
